# SKL-WRI — Writing Style

## Trigger

Activate this skill when drafting or revising any long-form content for Paphos Advisors: guides, FAQs, checklists, comparison articles, or news updates. This skill governs how to construct sentences, paragraphs, sections, and articles. Read alongside SKL-TOV — they are complementary, not interchangeable. SKL-TOV defines the voice. SKL-WRI defines the craft.

---

## Opening an Article

The first paragraph must earn the reader's attention by stating what the article delivers and why it matters to them now. It does not warm up. It does not explain what the article is "going to cover." It starts with the point.

**Do this:**
> "If you meet four conditions — spending at least 60 days in Cyprus, not spending more than 183 days in any other single country, maintaining a permanent home in Cyprus, and holding a Cyprus employment or directorship through 31 December — you qualify as a Cyprus tax resident for that year."

**Not this:**
> "In this guide, we will be exploring what the 60-day rule means, how it works, and what you need to do to qualify."

The second and third sentences provide context: who this is for, what changes in their situation once they act on this information. No more than three sentences before the first heading or the first useful piece of information.

---

## Paragraph Construction

One idea per paragraph. A paragraph that covers two ideas should be two paragraphs.

**Ideal paragraph structure:**
1. Lead sentence — states the point
2. Supporting sentence(s) — explain, qualify, or evidence the point
3. Consequence sentence (optional) — what this means for the reader

Maximum four sentences for most paragraphs. If a paragraph runs to six or more sentences, it either contains two ideas (split it) or is over-explaining (cut it).

Short paragraphs are not a problem. A single strong sentence can be its own paragraph when the point is important enough to stand alone.

## Section Openings and Closings

**Section openings:** The first sentence of each H2 section must stand alone as a summary of that section. A reader skimming headings and first sentences should get the full picture of the article without reading every word.

**Section closings:** Do not close a section with a summary of what was just covered. Either end with the most important point of the section, or end with a forward link to what comes next. Never write "As we have seen..." or "Now that we understand X, let us look at Y."

---

## Transitions

Transitions between sections should be invisible. The structure — headings, numbered steps, logical sequence — does the navigational work. You do not need to announce that you are moving on.

**Cut these phrases entirely:**
- "Moving on to..."
- "Now let us turn to..."
- "Having covered X, we can now look at Y..."
- "As mentioned above..."
- "As we will see later..."

If a connection between sections genuinely needs signalling, use a single bridging sentence at the end of a section or the start of the next — not both.

---

## Writing Process Steps

Numbered steps for any sequential process. Each step follows this pattern:

**[Step number]: [Verb-led instruction]**
[What to do, in one to two sentences. Be specific about who does what and what the outcome is.]
[Supporting detail: documents needed, timeframe, where to go, common variation. No more than three sentences.]

Rules:
- Every step opens with a verb: "Apply at...", "Gather...", "Submit...", "Wait for..."
- Never open a step with "You need to..." — this is hedged and passive
- If a step has a sub-step or exception, use an indented bullet under the step — do not create H4 headings for minor variations
- Include the expected outcome at the end of multi-step processes: "Once complete, you will have..."

## Writing FAQs

Each question must be a real question — the exact phrasing a person would type into Google or say to an adviser. Test every question against this: would a client actually say this, or does it only make sense from the inside?

**Real question:** "Do I need to live in Cyprus full-time to be a tax resident?"
**Internal question:** "What are the residency criteria for tax purposes?"

Answer structure:
- First sentence: direct answer (yes, no, or the key number/fact)
- Second and third sentences: the essential context or caveat
- Final sentence (if needed): what the reader should do next

Fifty to 150 words per answer. If the answer needs more than 150 words, the question is too broad — split it.

---

## Writing Comparisons

Comparison articles have one job: help the reader make a decision. Every section should serve that job.

Rules:
- Open with a Quick Comparison table. Readers scan before they read.
- Each country/option section must be parallel in structure — same sub-headings, same order. A reader should be able to move horizontally across sections to compare like for like.
- The Decision Guide section ("Choose X if...") must be specific and binary. "Choose Cyprus if you want low tax" is vague. "Choose Cyprus if you cannot or will not spend more than 183 days in any one country" is usable.
- State the limitations of the comparison: where figures come from, what changes frequently, what requires local advice.

## Handling Complex or Technical Information

Never simplify by omitting. If a rule has exceptions, state the exceptions. If a process has edge cases, note them — even if briefly.

The way to handle complexity without overwhelming the reader:

1. State the default case first: what applies to most readers
2. Then flag the exception: "If [specific circumstance], the process differs — [brief explanation or link]"
3. Use a `[REVIEW: ...]` flag for anything that may have changed recently

Tables work well for parallel technical information (fee schedules, document lists, timeline comparisons). Use them whenever the information has two or more attributes per item.

Do not use tables for information that flows naturally as prose. A table with one column is a list. Write it as a list.

## Caveats and Disclaimers

Caveats are necessary. They are not apologies. The difference:

**Apology caveat (wrong):** "Of course, this is quite complex and everyone's situation is different, so it's really hard to give definitive guidance."

**Functional caveat (right):** "Processing times vary. The figures above reflect typical cases in 2026 — confirm current timescales with the relevant department before planning around them."

Rules:
- State the caveat once, specifically, at the point where it is relevant
- Do not repeat the same caveat in multiple places
- Never open a section with a caveat — it signals that the content that follows cannot be trusted
- The standard disclaimer at the end of every regulatory article is mandatory and is not a caveat — do not summarise or abbreviate it

---

## Closings

End with a clear next step, not a summary. The reader has just read the article — they do not need a recap. They need to know what to do.

A good closing:
- Names the specific next action
- Makes it low-friction: a phone number, a booking link, a checklist to download
- Restates the main value proposition in one sentence (not a summary of content)

**Not this:** "In this guide, we have covered the four conditions for Cyprus tax residency, the 2026 dual residency change, the step-by-step process, and the common mistakes to avoid."

**This:** "If you are close to satisfying the four conditions, speaking to a Cyprus tax specialist before 31 December is the decision that determines whether the year counts. Book a free 30-minute consultation to confirm your position."

## Avoiding AI Writing Patterns

These patterns mark content as machine-generated and undermine the Paphos Advisor voice. Flag and fix any of these on review:

| Pattern | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Throat-clearing openers | "In today's complex regulatory landscape..." | Cut. Start with the point. |
| Symmetrical bullet rhythm | Every bullet is exactly 8–12 words | Vary length; some bullets should be 4 words, some 15 |
| Stacked adjectives | "comprehensive, practical, and actionable guide" | One adjective maximum, or none |
| Fake balance | "On one hand X. On the other hand Y. Ultimately it depends." | Take a position. State the recommendation. |
| Conclusion restatement | Final paragraph summarises every section | Replace with a single next step |
| Echo headings | H2 says "Benefits of X" and the first sentence says "There are several benefits of X" | First sentence adds information not already in the heading |
| Passive attribution | "It is recommended that..." / "It is important to note..." | Name who recommends it or cut the recommendation hedge |
| Universal hedges | "Generally speaking", "In most cases", "Typically" used in every paragraph | Reserve for genuine uncertainty; cut when the statement is simply true |

## Quality Check

Before passing any draft for human review, confirm:

- [ ] First paragraph states the main point — no warm-up
- [ ] Each paragraph contains one idea
- [ ] Section openings summarise the section in one sentence
- [ ] No transition phrases announcing a change of topic
- [ ] Process steps open with a verb and include the expected outcome
- [ ] FAQ questions read like real client questions
- [ ] Caveats are specific and placed at the point of relevance — not at the opening
- [ ] Closing ends with a next step, not a summary
- [ ] No AI writing patterns from the table above
- [ ] Tables used for parallel technical information; prose used for connected ideas
