# Business Formation FAQs

Covers Cyprus company formation, banking, structure, and the self-employed vs company decision.

---

## Q: Can I form a Cyprus company without being in Cyprus?

**Short answer:** Yes. The full company formation process can be handled remotely by a Cyprus lawyer. Physical presence is not required for incorporation.

A Cyprus private limited company can be formed entirely remotely. Your Cyprus lawyer handles all filings with the Registrar of Companies via the DRCOR eFiling portal. You provide certified copies of your passport and proof of address, review and sign the Memorandum and Articles of Association and consent forms electronically or by post, and your lawyer submits the full pack to the Registrar. You do not need to be physically present in Cyprus for the incorporation itself. You will eventually need to be in Cyprus to satisfy the 60-day tax residency requirements (if that is your objective), but the company can be registered in advance of your arrival.

## Q: Does a Cyprus company need a local Cypriot director?

**Short answer:** Not legally. You can be the sole director regardless of nationality. However, a local nominee director may help establish tax substance if you spend little time in Cyprus.

There is no legal requirement for a Cyprus company to have a local Cypriot director. You can be the sole director regardless of your nationality or country of residence. However, where board decisions are made determines where the company is considered tax resident — the concept of place of effective management. If you are frequently in Cyprus and managing the company from Cyprus, this is naturally satisfied. If you are rarely in Cyprus, a local nominee director can help establish tax substance. Nominee director services typically cost €1,000–€3,000 per year. For most relocating entrepreneurs who will themselves be Cyprus tax residents, a nominee director is unnecessary — discuss with your tax adviser.

## Q: What is the minimum share capital required to form a Cyprus private limited company?

**Short answer:** There is no statutory minimum. A Cyprus Ltd can be incorporated with €1, though €1,000 is typical in practice.

Cyprus private limited companies have no statutory minimum share capital requirement — a company can technically be incorporated with a share capital of €1. In practice, €1,000 is a common starting point as it provides flexibility and satisfies the practical expectations of banks and counterparties during KYC. The government registration fee is based on authorised share capital (€105 for capital up to €5,000 as of April 2026 — verify with your lawyer at point of application). Share capital can be increased after incorporation if your business requires it.

## Q: How long does it take to open a corporate bank account in Cyprus?

**Short answer:** 4–12 weeks, sometimes longer. Budget 2–3 months from instruction to a functioning account. The bank account is the main bottleneck in Cyprus company formation.

Opening a corporate bank account in Cyprus typically takes 4–12 weeks, and can take longer for complex ownership structures, non-EU shareholders, or clients with higher-risk business profiles. Common causes of delay: incomplete documentation, source of funds queries, or requests for additional information about the business activity. To minimise delays: prepare a comprehensive KYC pack upfront (certified passports, proof of address, source of funds, business description, personal bank statements for 3–6 months), use an experienced local lawyer as an introducer to the bank, and build 2–3 months of buffer into your timeline.

## Q: Can I use a Wise or Revolut Business account instead of a Cyprus bank account for my company?

**Short answer:** As an interim option only. Wise and Revolut Business are EMI accounts (not bank accounts) and may not be accepted by all counterparties. Confirm with your accountant.

Wise Business and Revolut Business are Electronic Money Institution (EMI) accounts — they are not bank accounts and are not regulated in the same way as licensed banks. They are being used by some Cyprus companies as interim solutions while waiting for a full Cyprus bank account to be approved (which can take 4–12 weeks or more). EMI accounts allow you to receive payments, pay suppliers, and hold company funds while the bank application progresses. However, some clients, counterparties, and applications may not accept an EMI account in place of a bank account. Always confirm with your accountant and any key counterparties whether an EMI account is acceptable for your specific purposes before relying on it as your primary business account.

## Q: Should I set up a Cyprus company or operate as a self-employed individual?

**Short answer:** For income below €60–70k, self-employment is often more efficient. Above that threshold, a Cyprus company with dividend extraction usually delivers a better overall tax position.

The right structure depends on your income level and business activity. As a self-employed individual in Cyprus, you pay income tax at progressive rates and social insurance contributions. For lower income levels — broadly below €60–70k per year — the administrative simplicity and lower cost of self-employment often outweighs the tax benefit of a company structure. Above that threshold, a Cyprus private limited company combined with non-dom status and dividend extraction typically delivers a significantly better overall tax position. The effective rate on profits extracted as dividends (12.5% corporate tax plus 2.65% GHS levy) is lower than the combined income tax and social insurance burden at higher self-employment income levels. Always take specific advice from a Cyprus tax specialist before choosing your structure.

## Q: Can I open a Cyprus bank account for a cryptocurrency business?

**Short answer:** No. Cyprus-regulated banks will not open accounts for companies whose primary activity is cryptocurrency. EMI accounts (Wise Business, Revolut Business) are the main working alternative.

All major Cyprus banks — Bank of Cyprus, Hellenic Bank, and Alpha Bank — apply a blanket policy of refusing to open accounts for companies whose primary activity is cryptocurrency trading, exchange, or custody. This applies regardless of whether the company holds or is seeking a Cyprus licence. The refusal is driven by AML and CFT risk concerns and correspondent banking restrictions. The practical alternative is an Electronic Money Institution (EMI) account. Some crypto companies maintain EMI accounts for operations and hold reserves in offshore accounts in more crypto-friendly jurisdictions. Regulatory frameworks for crypto in Cyprus are evolving — seek specialist advice on the current position for your specific business model.
